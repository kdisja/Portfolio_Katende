## Three core stories to rehearse

Use **Context → Responsibility → Actions → Result → Reflection**. Keep each answer to about two minutes unless they invite more detail. Add a specific outcome or decision where you can substantiate one; do not imply impact that you cannot verify.

### 1. Malawi disease surveillance and spatial analysis

**Use for:** Evaluation design, translating findings into action, communicating technical results, working with imperfect or geographically distributed data.

- **Context:** Onchocerciasis mass drug administration across 238 community clusters in Southern Malawi.
- **Your work:** Integrated population-based survey and geospatial data; assessed treatment coverage and geographic inequalities; examined spatial patterns of persistent under-coverage using Global and Local Moran’s I, neighbourhood analysis, and multivariable regression.
- **Communication/action link:** Developed an interactive Shiny surveillance and decision-support dashboard to display coverage patterns and help identify priority communities for microplanning.
- **Point to emphasize:** Begin with the program decision the evidence was meant to inform. Explain how you checked and interpreted the data, and how users could act on the results.
- **Prepare to clarify:** What you personally designed or built; how you handled missingness, spatial units, or data-quality concerns; who used the outputs; and what changed as a result.

### 2. Canadian linked-data research

**Use for:** Data governance, analytical rigor, managing complexity, prioritizing quality and reproducibility.

- **Context:** Research on air pollution exposure and cancer incidence using national survey, cancer registry, and Canadian Census Health and Environment Cohort data.
- **Your work:** Helped construct analytical datasets exceeding 100 million records in a secure Statistics Canada cloud environment; developed standardized ICD-coded definitions for eight cancer outcomes; used survival, multilevel, and geospatial methods.
- **Point to emphasize:** The record count is not the achievement by itself. Explain how clear definitions, data linkage, validation, privacy controls, and reproducible analysis made the results credible.
- **Prepare to clarify:** Your exact ownership, one specific data-quality or coding challenge, how you validated a key step, and how you communicated uncertainty or limitations.

### 3. Humanitarian nutrition and public-health programming

**Use for:** Stakeholder engagement, adapting evaluation to context, field operations, and practical use of evidence.

- **Context:** Nutrition, food security, and public-health work in Uganda, including refugee and vulnerable communities in West Nile and Karamoja.
- **Your work:** Designed or supported surveys, data-collection tools, and analysis plans; contributed to SMART nutrition surveillance and IPC-aligned food-security assessments; used GIS-based surveillance to identify malnutrition hotspots and inform targeting.
- **Point to emphasize:** Evaluation has to work for the people collecting and using the data. Describe how you coordinated with partners or field teams and balanced rigor with feasibility and local context.
- **Prepare to clarify:** Which assessment components you personally led; how findings were shared; and an example of adapting an approach to field constraints.

## Likely questions and response approaches

### “How would you build an evaluation framework for one of our programs?”

Walk through a clear sequence:

1. Clarify the program’s purpose, intended users, and the decisions the evaluation needs to support.
2. Agree on evaluation questions and a practical theory of change or logic model.
3. Select measures and data sources that fit the questions; define them consistently.
4. Check data quality, feasibility, privacy, and relevant validity and reliability evidence.
5. Plan analysis in advance, including subgroup comparisons where appropriate and sufficiently supported.
6. Interpret findings with program and subject-matter partners; distinguish evidence from assumptions.
7. Communicate limitations and recommendations in usable formats, then agree how actions and outcomes will be followed up.

For assessment programs, add that validity concerns the interpretation and intended use of scores—not just the test in isolation. Ask what evidence Touchstone currently uses to examine reliability, fairness, accessibility, and consequences. Do not present yourself as a psychometrician if that is not your background; explain how you would partner with psychometric and content experts.

### “How do you make sure findings are trustworthy?”

Use an example, then cover a few concrete practices: precise definitions, validation checks, documentation, appropriate methods, privacy safeguards, transparent limitations, and review with relevant subject-matter partners. Explain how you would investigate an unexpected result rather than automatically treating it as real.

### “How would you assess fairness or differences between groups?”

Start by clarifying the decision and the groups that matter. Check whether data are complete and comparable, whether subgroup sizes support interpretation, and whether differences may reflect access, process, context, or measurement—not just individual performance. Use appropriate disaggregation, protect privacy, communicate uncertainty, and involve people with relevant expertise. Avoid claiming that a single statistical test establishes fairness.

### “Tell us about a time your work influenced a decision.”

Choose the Malawi dashboard or humanitarian assessment only if you can explain a real use or decision. State what the evidence showed, who needed it, how you communicated it, what action followed, and how you know. If the work informed recommendations but you cannot confirm implementation, say that directly.

### “How do you manage competing priorities?”

Give a specific example if possible. Describe how you clarified deadlines and decision impact, estimated effort and dependencies, agreed on trade-offs with stakeholders, protected essential quality checks, and communicated changes early. For a manager role, make visible how you support team focus—not only how you personally work faster.

### “What is your leadership or people-management experience?”

Be candid about formal direct-report experience. Then give concrete evidence of leadership: coordinating a project, setting analytical direction, training or supporting colleagues, aligning partners, reviewing work, or communicating decisions. Explain how you would set expectations, provide feedback, and help a team deliver consistently.

### “What would you need to learn in this role?”

A credible answer could be Touchstone’s specific assessment programs, current evaluation questions, internal data structures, and how assessment validity and fairness are currently examined. Pair the learning need with a plan: listen to program and technical experts, review existing evidence and measures, understand stakeholder needs, then identify priorities together.

## Manager-level signals to demonstrate

- You start with the organizational decision, not with a favorite method or tool.
- You can explain technical findings clearly without overstating certainty.
- You build repeatable processes and clear standards, not one-off analyses alone.
- You collaborate with program, assessment, technical, and leadership partners.
- You know when to disaggregate data and when the evidence is too limited.
- You protect confidentiality and explain how data are governed.
- You can prioritize work, delegate appropriately, and communicate risks early.
- You are open about where you would collaborate or build new expertise.
