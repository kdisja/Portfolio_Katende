Student Wellness Evaluation Audio Note

Good afternoon, and thank you for having me. Over the next ten minutes, I’ll walk through how I would approach this issue as Evaluation Lead. I’ll explain how I would frame the problem, how I would bring the data together responsibly, how I would build an evaluation and improvement framework, and what I would advise leadership to do now and later.

The challenge is that we have three sources of evidence on student mental health support: the Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey, student satisfaction data, and Student Wellness service data. All three point to increased demand for mental health support, but they do not measure the same thing. They differ in definitions, methods, populations, collection periods, and reporting.

Leadership has asked five questions. The rest of this discussion answers them in order. So let’s start by looking at what each of these data sources is actually telling us.

I think of these as three different lenses. The Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey measures need across all students, including those who never use our services. The satisfaction survey measures the experience of students who respond. And service data measures demand and use: who comes through the door and why.

Each one has a blind spot. The Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey is a self-reported snapshot on its own cycle. Satisfaction data misses non-users and students who gave up. Service data cannot see students who never come in.

For example, the Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey might point to financial stress, while intake data shows academic stress. That doesn’t mean one is wrong. Intake often captures the immediate trigger, while the survey captures broader drivers. My approach is triangulation. Where flawed sources agree, like rising demand, we can be more confident. Where they disagree, like on causes, we investigate rather than pick one.

And that leads to an important distinction: demand is not the same as need.

This funnel shows the journey from a student who is struggling, to becoming aware of services, seeking help, being seen, and hopefully improving. Students drop out at every step. The Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey sees the top of the funnel. Service data sees the middle. And right now, nothing systematically measures the bottom: whether students improve.

So when visits go up, that could mean more students are struggling. But it could also mean less stigma, more counsellors, easier booking, enrolment growth, the same students returning more often, or simply a change in how visits are recorded. To tell these apart, I would look at unique students rather than visits, rates per 1,000 enrolled students, a log of staffing and system changes, and whether Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey distress scores moved in the same direction.

So given all that, what can we actually conclude from the current data?

What we can conclude is that demand pressure is real. Three independent sources agree that students are asking for more support. That is enough evidence to act on access now.

What we cannot conclude yet is what is causing the increase, what the true prevalence is from service data alone, whether services improve outcomes, whether trends are real if definitions changed, and what patterns look like for smaller student groups.

One important point: satisfaction is not an outcome. A student can be satisfied with their counsellor and still be struggling.

What we need to add is a clearer picture of unique clients versus repeat visits, wait times and capacity, an outcome measure at intake and follow-up, consistent equity data, and direct student voice, especially from non-users.

So in short, we know enough to act on access now, but not enough to know what is driving need or whether our services improve outcomes. That second gap is what an evaluation framework fixes.

Before we can build that framework, though, the data needs to speak the same language.

I would approach data integration in four steps. First, an inventory of every source: who is included, what is measured, when, and how it is reported. Second, a shared data dictionary and a change log, recording when forms, systems, or staffing changed, so we can tell real trends from recording artifacts.

Third, harmonize what can be harmonized: align to the academic term, use rates per 1,000 students, and clearly flag anything that cannot be compared. And fourth, rate the quality of each source openly, so leadership knows how much weight each one can carry.

The guiding principle is to integrate at the aggregate level first, and link individual records only when there is a clear question it answers, with a privacy impact assessment and governance agreement in place.

That brings me to governance.

Mental health data is among the most sensitive data an institution holds. I would work within FIPPA and PHIPA for clinical records, and use only the minimum data needed: de-identified extracts, with clinical notes staying with clinicians.

That also means small-cell suppression so no student can be identified, named data stewards, and clear sharing agreements. Equity data should be voluntary, clearly explained, and co-designed with student communities. And we should be transparent with students about what we collect and what changed as a result.

With good data in place, the next question is how we monitor whether services are making a difference.

The evaluation framework starts with a logic model. Inputs like staff and programs lead to activities like counselling and triage, which produce outputs like students seen. But the real goal is on the right: short-term outcomes like reduced distress, and long-term outcomes like wellbeing, retention, and student success.

Right now, most of our data stops at outputs. So I would track a small set of indicators in four areas: access, such as wait time to first appointment; quality, such as satisfaction; outcomes, using a short validated measure at intake and follow-up; and equity, meaning every indicator broken down by student group where the numbers allow.

The single most important addition is that outcome measure, because it is the only way to know whether our services actually help.

And a framework only matters if it drives improvement.

I would use Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles. For example, if wait times are the main pressure, we might pilot same-day triage for one term, measure wait times, repeat bookings, and outcomes before and after, then decide to adopt, adapt, or drop it.

On the reporting side, I would use a monthly operational dashboard for the service team, a term-by-term summary for leadership, and an annual report that brings in each new Canadian Campus Wellbeing Survey cycle.

So what does this mean for leadership?

My advice is in three phases.

Now, in the first six months: a data inventory and change log, shared definitions, a simple access dashboard, triage to ease wait times, and three new intake fields: a unique ID, wait time, and an outcome measure.

Next, from six to eighteen months: a baseline indicator set, focus groups with students who do not use our services, a first equity analysis, and pilots with evaluation built in.

Later, from eighteen months to three years: an integrated framework across all sources, governed record linkage only if it is justified, an annual public report, and partnerships across campus, because some drivers, like finances and academics, sit outside Student Wellness.

Throughout, I would be transparent about confidence: this we know, this we suspect, and this we don’t know yet.

I’d like to finish by sharing why this problem feels familiar to me.

In my current work with the World Health Organization, I analyzed an ivermectin mass drug administration coverage survey in Southern Malawi across more than 8,000 people in 238 clusters. Overall uptake was 71.6 percent, below the 80 percent target. But 99.8 percent of people who were offered the drug took it.

About 28 percent were untreated, and when we asked those who were not reached why, the main reasons were not knowing about the campaign and being absent. Only 1.4 percent refused.

So the problem was not unwillingness. It was reach. And the same lesson applies here: high satisfaction among students who use Wellness can hide students who never reach it.

Alongside that, I bring data linkage experience from CAREX, OCAP-compliant governance from the First Nations Health Authority, student wellbeing survey work from UBC’s Campus Food Security project, and dashboard and community advisory experience.

To summarize, demand is rising, but the causes are not yet clear. We should harmonize and govern the data before combining it. We should measure outcomes and equity, not just activity. And we should act on access now while building evidence in parallel.

Thank you. I’d welcome your questions.
