Question. Explain the Five Safes framework you referenced in your questionnaire.

I think of the Five Safes as a structured way of managing disclosure risk while still enabling useful research. The five dimensions are safe people, safe projects, safe settings, safe data and safe outputs.

Safe people means ensuring that individuals accessing sensitive data are appropriately authorized and understand their responsibilities. Safe projects means that the use of the data is legitimate, approved and aligned with the stated purpose. Safe settings refers to conducting the work in an appropriately controlled environment. Safe data means managing the level of identifiability and limiting information to what is necessary. Safe outputs means reviewing results before release so that tables, figures or other products do not inadvertently disclose confidential information.

In my Canadian linked-data work, these principles are practical rather than theoretical. They affect who can access the data, where analysis occurs, which variables are available, how analytical files are constructed and what can leave the secure environment. I would bring the same privacy-by-design mindset to sensitive student wellness data, while following the University's specific governance policies and applicable legislation.

Follow-up question. What is safe output?

A safe output is a result that has been checked so it does not disclose or enable inference about identifiable individuals. That can involve minimum cell-size rules, suppressing complementary cells, limiting overly granular cross-tabulations, reviewing extreme values and ensuring that combinations of variables cannot reveal a person.

Follow-up question. How would you handle small cells?

I would follow the University's disclosure rules, which may require suppression, aggregation or broader reporting categories. I would also consider statistical stability, not only confidentiality. If a subgroup is important but cannot be reported quantitatively, I would look for another responsible way to represent the issue without exposing individuals.

Follow-up question. What if a senior leader asks for identifiable data?

I would clarify the purpose and authority for the request and determine whether identifiable information is actually necessary. If aggregated or de-identified information can answer the question, I would use that. If the request is outside approved access or purpose, I would involve the appropriate privacy or governance office rather than release the data based on seniority.

Question. How would you handle a senior leader requesting identifiable student-level information?

I would first clarify the purpose of the request and the decision the leader is trying to make. Seniority alone would not determine whether identifiable data should be released.

I would assess whether the requested information is authorized for that purpose and whether the decision can be supported with less identifiable information. In many cases, aggregated or appropriately de-identified data may answer the operational question without exposing individual-level information.

If the request fell outside the approved purpose, access permissions or privacy requirements, I would explain the limitation clearly and work with the appropriate privacy or data-governance contacts to identify a compliant alternative. My goal would be to support the decision while applying data minimization, purpose limitation and appropriate disclosure controls.

Follow-up question. What if the request is urgent?

Urgency changes the turnaround time, not the privacy standard. I would quickly clarify the decision need, provide the least identifiable information that can answer it, and escalate promptly to the appropriate privacy or governance contact if authorization is uncertain.

Follow-up question. How do you balance access and privacy?

I use a proportional approach: provide enough information for the legitimate purpose while minimizing identifiability and unnecessary access. Good governance should enable appropriate use, not simply restrict data. The key is matching the level of detail and access to the decision and the user's authorization.

Question. Tell us about your experience with R, SAS and SQL.

Question. How do you translate complex analysis for non-technical leaders?

I start with the decision the audience needs to make rather than the statistical method I used. In my Malawi work, for example, regression and spatial statistics were important for scientific validity, but program managers did not need a technical lecture on every coefficient or spatial statistic.

They needed to know where coverage was lower, which populations or communities appeared underserved, what barriers might explain the pattern, how confident we were in the findings and what actions the evidence supported. I translated the analysis into maps, concise summaries and an interactive dashboard that allowed stakeholders to explore geographic patterns.

I generally use layered communication: a clear headline and implications for leadership, accessible visualizations for exploration, and technical documentation for those who need methodological detail. My goal is to simplify the communication without oversimplifying the science.

Follow-up question. How would you present an inconclusive result?

I would say clearly that the evidence is inconclusive, show the effect estimate and uncertainty, and explain why certainty is limited-for example sample size, measurement or design. I would still identify what the result rules out, what remains plausible, and what additional evidence would most improve the decision.

Follow-up question. What if leadership wants one number?

I would give them the most decision-relevant headline number if one is defensible, but pair it with the minimum context needed to interpret it-usually the denominator, comparison, time period and uncertainty. I would avoid giving a precise-looking number that answers the wrong question.

Follow-up question. How do you tailor communication to different audiences?

I keep the underlying analysis consistent but change the level of detail. Leadership gets the decision, key evidence, uncertainty and recommended action; operational teams get enough detail to understand where and how to act; technical audiences receive methods, assumptions and diagnostics. I use the same evidence with different layers of explanation.

Question. How would you design a dashboard for Student Wellness leadership?

I would start with decisions, not charts. I would meet with leadership and operational users to identify the questions they need the dashboard to answer and which actions could follow from the information.

For Student Wellness, useful domains might include service demand and utilization, unique students served, access and wait times, capacity, referral pathways, program reach, selected outcomes, student experience and equity indicators. I would distinguish operational indicators that need frequent monitoring from longer-term evaluation outcomes.

I would establish consistent metric definitions and denominators, include trend context rather than isolated numbers, and build appropriate privacy protections such as suppression or aggregation for small cells. I would also avoid overloading the dashboard; the most important indicators should be immediately visible, with drill-down available when useful.

Finally, I would test the dashboard with intended users and revisit it as decision needs change. A good dashboard is not simply visually attractive - it should make the next question or decision easier.

Follow-up question. Which KPIs would you prioritize?

I would prioritize a small set tied to decisions: unique students served, demand relative to capacity, wait time, service completion, referral flow, selected outcomes or experience measures, and equity in reach and timeliness. The exact set should follow Student Wellness priorities and established definitions rather than my arriving with a fixed dashboard.

Follow-up question. How would you prevent misinterpretation?

I would use clear metric definitions, appropriate denominators, trend context and annotations for major operational changes. I would avoid ambiguous percentages, flag suppressed or unstable estimates, and provide drill-down or documentation so users can understand what a metric does and does not mean.

Follow-up question. What visualization tools have you used?

I have used R extensively for publication and decision-support graphics, ArcGIS Pro and QGIS for geospatial visualization, and Shiny for interactive dashboards. I also have exposure to BI tools such as Power BI and Tableau. I focus on the decision and audience first, then choose the tool that fits the environment.
