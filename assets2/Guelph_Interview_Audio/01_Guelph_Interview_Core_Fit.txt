Question. Your questionnaire mentions your experience as an international student. How would that inform your work without assuming your experience represents all students?

My own experience gives me an appreciation for the fact that students can experience the same institution very differently, but I would never use my personal experience as evidence of what other students need. In fact, it reinforces for me why rigorous evaluation is important.

I would combine administrative and service data with surveys and, where appropriate, qualitative evidence to understand who is accessing services, who may not be represented in the data, and whether access or outcomes differ across student populations. I would also involve students and service providers in interpreting findings, because numbers need context.

So my experience gives me motivation and perspective, but the recommendations I make would remain grounded in systematic evidence, appropriate denominators, careful interpretation and engagement with the populations the services are intended to support.

Follow-up question. How would you measure equity?

I would compare access and outcomes relative to an appropriate measure of need or the eligible student population, rather than relying on raw counts. Depending on the program, I would examine reach, wait time, completion, experience and outcomes across relevant groups, while considering intersectionality, missing demographic information, uncertainty and privacy.

Follow-up question. What if sample sizes are small?

I would avoid overinterpreting unstable estimates. I would consider aggregation across time or clinically and operationally meaningful categories, report uncertainty, suppress cells where privacy requires it, and use qualitative evidence where appropriate. I would not combine groups simply to make the statistics look cleaner if doing so erased meaningful differences.

Follow-up question. How would you avoid stigmatizing groups?

I would frame differences around systems, access and modifiable barriers rather than treating characteristics of a student group as the problem. I would use neutral language, provide context and denominators, involve relevant stakeholders in interpretation where appropriate, and avoid reporting granular results when they could identify or unfairly label a small population.

Question. Tell us about your current role and the impact you have driven.

In my current role as a research epidemiologist supporting work with WHO in Malawi, I analyse population-based survey and geospatial data to assess the performance of onchocerciasis mass drug administration across communities in Southern Malawi.

The key analytical challenge was that an overall coverage estimate could hide important geographic differences. I therefore developed a reproducible workflow in R and ArcGIS Pro, assessed receipt, uptake and compliance, examined determinants of uptake, and used spatial methods to identify areas with lower coverage.

The impact was shifting the conversation from "What is the overall coverage?" to "Where are the gaps, what is contributing to them, and where should follow-up resources be prioritized?" I also developed an interactive dashboard that allows stakeholders to explore district and community-level patterns rather than relying only on static tables.

That experience reflects how I approach analytics generally: start with the decision that needs to be made, ensure the data are reliable, use methods appropriate to the question, and translate the results into a form that supports action.

Follow-up question. What exactly was your individual contribution?

I led the analytical workflow: defining the treatment indicators, cleaning and structuring the survey data, conducting the regression and spatial analyses, producing the maps, and developing the interactive dashboard. I also translated the results into program-relevant findings about where coverage was lower and what barriers could guide targeted follow-up.

Follow-up question. How did stakeholders use the dashboard?

The dashboard was designed to move stakeholders beyond a single overall coverage estimate. It allowed them to explore district and cluster-level variation, identify lower-coverage areas and examine patterns relevant to microplanning and follow-up. The practical value was making the analysis usable without requiring stakeholders to work through statistical output.

Follow-up question. What did you do when the data had limitations?

I made the limitations part of the analysis rather than hiding them. I checked missingness and denominator definitions, documented exclusions and assumptions, used methods appropriate to the clustered survey structure, and distinguished exploratory spatial signals from findings that remained statistically robust after adjustment. I then communicated what the data could and could not support.
