Question. How would you evaluate a new Student Wellness program?

I would begin with the decision the evaluation needs to support rather than starting with the available data. First, I would work with the program team and relevant stakeholders to understand the intervention, its objectives, target population and theory of change. I would develop or review a logic model connecting inputs and activities to outputs and intended short- and longer-term outcomes.

Second, I would translate that into specific evaluation questions. For a mental-health initiative, for example, we might ask whether the intended students are being reached, whether access or wait times have improved, whether students report improved outcomes, and whether access and outcomes differ across student populations.

I would then identify indicators and data sources. Depending on the program, that could include administrative service data, program records, surveys and qualitative feedback from students or providers. I would assess data quality and establish an appropriate baseline or comparison. The design could range from descriptive monitoring to pre/post analysis, interrupted time series or another quasi-experimental approach, depending on implementation and data availability.

Equity would be built into the evaluation from the beginning. I would examine who is accessing the program relative to the population experiencing need, not simply compare raw counts. Finally, I would translate the findings into recommendations and an appropriate knowledge product - for example, an operational dashboard, evaluation report or concise leadership briefing.

My overall sequence is: understand the program, clarify the decision, define evaluation questions, establish indicators, assess the data, analyse rigorously, interpret with stakeholders, and translate the evidence into action.

Question. Scenario: Counselling utilization increased by 30% this year. Leadership asks you to explain why. What would you do?

Once I was confident the signal was real, I would decompose it. I would also use an appropriate denominator because raw counts could be misleading if enrolment or the population eligible for the service changed.

Next, I would examine whether the increase is concentrated in particular services or student populations, while protecting confidentiality and avoiding unstable estimates from very small cells. I would then investigate context: new services, expanded hours, outreach campaigns, changes in referral practices or other operational changes that could increase access.

Question. How do you ensure data quality and consistency across multiple systems?

I treat data quality as part of the entire analytical lifecycle rather than a one-time cleaning exercise. I begin by establishing expectations: record counts, unique identifiers, permissible values, date ranges, variable definitions and relationships between tables.

I then assess completeness, duplicates, missingness, outliers, inconsistent coding and logical inconsistencies. When combining systems, I explicitly check join cardinality and reconcile counts before and after joins because an incorrect many-to-many join can produce plausible-looking but incorrect results.

I also examine changes over time. A sudden increase in an indicator could represent a real change, but it could also reflect a new reporting definition, a system migration, expanded service capacity or improved data completeness. I document transformations and assumptions and, where possible, automate reproducible validation checks.

My guiding principle is that a sophisticated statistical model cannot compensate for poorly understood data. Before interpreting a result, I want to understand where the data came from, what they actually measure and what limitations they carry.

Question. Explain the Five Safes framework you referenced in your questionnaire.

I think of the Five Safes as a structured way of managing disclosure risk while still enabling useful research. The five dimensions are safe people, safe projects, safe settings, safe data and safe outputs.

Safe people means ensuring that individuals accessing sensitive data are appropriately authorized and understand their responsibilities. Safe projects means that the use of the data is legitimate, approved and aligned with the stated purpose. Safe settings refers to conducting the work in an appropriately controlled environment. Safe data means managing the level of identifiability and limiting information to what is necessary. Safe outputs means reviewing results before release so that tables, figures or other products do not inadvertently disclose confidential information.

In my Canadian linked-data work, these principles are practical rather than theoretical. They affect who can access the data, where analysis occurs, which variables are available, how analytical files are constructed and what can leave the secure environment. I would bring the same privacy-by-design mindset to sensitive student wellness data, while following the University's specific governance policies and applicable legislation.

Follow-up question. What is safe output?

A safe output is a result that has been checked so it does not disclose or enable inference about identifiable individuals. That can involve minimum cell-size rules, suppressing complementary cells, limiting overly granular cross-tabulations, reviewing extreme values and ensuring that combinations of variables cannot reveal a person.

Follow-up question. How would you handle small cells?

I would follow the University's disclosure rules, which may require suppression, aggregation or broader reporting categories. I would also consider statistical stability, not only confidentiality. If a subgroup is important but cannot be reported quantitatively, I would look for another responsible way to represent the issue without exposing individuals.

Follow-up question. What if a senior leader asks for identifiable data?

Question. Tell us about a time a stakeholder disagreed with your findings or interpretation.

When there is disagreement, my first step is to understand whether the disagreement is about the data, the method, the interpretation or the operational implications. I try not to become defensive because stakeholders often have contextual knowledge that can improve the analysis.

I would walk through the definitions, assumptions and evidence transparently, invite the stakeholder to explain what they believe is missing, and test reasonable alternative explanations or sensitivity analyses where appropriate. If the evidence changes, I update the conclusion. If it does not, I explain clearly what the data support and what they do not support.

The objective is not to "win" the disagreement. It is to reach the most defensible interpretation while preserving trust and scientific integrity.

Follow-up question. Can you give us a concrete example?

In applied public-health work, stakeholders can reasonably focus on an overall program indicator while the analysis shows meaningful variation underneath it. My approach has been to walk them through the denominator, geographic or population differences and the evidence behind the interpretation, then use maps or stratified summaries to make the pattern visible. That turns disagreement into a question we can test rather than a personal dispute.

Follow-up question. What if a leader pressures you to change a conclusion?

I would separate wording from evidence. I am happy to make language clearer, add context or test a reasonable alternative analysis, but I would not change a conclusion simply to make it more comfortable. I would document the analysis and explain what the data support, escalating through appropriate scientific or governance channels if necessary.

If asked for a weakness/growth area: One area I'm still building is direct grant-writing experience. If asked for a time something didn't go as planned: While working on the CAREX Canada / CanCHEC linkage examining air pollution and cancer outcomes, I discovered partway through analysis that the sex variable had been miscoded during data harmonization - effectively reversed relative to the source data dictionary. It surfaced because sex-specific results for some of the cancers we studied looked biologically implausible. Rather than assume the finding was real, I stopped, traced the discrepancy back through the original data dictionaries for each linked source, and found the recoding error. I corrected it, re-ran the affected analyses, and built an explicit validation check - cross-tabulating sex against sex-specific outcomes - into that pipeline going forward. It reinforced something I now do by default: check that a result makes biological and logical sense before trusting it, especially after linking or recoding data from multiple sources.

Follow-up question. What would have happened if you hadn't caught it?

The affected analysis could have produced a misleading interpretation and undermined confidence in later results. That is why I treat plausibility checks as part of quality assurance rather than assuming that code running without an error means the analysis is correct.

Follow-up question. How did you first notice something was off?

The pattern in the sex-specific results did not make sense relative to the outcome and the source documentation. That prompted me to trace the variable back through the harmonization steps rather than interpreting the unexpected result as a novel finding.

Follow-up question. What checks do you build in now to catch this earlier?

I build validation around every major transformation: frequency tables before and after recoding, cross-tabs against source variables, checks of expected ranges and missingness, row-count reconciliation after joins, and plausibility checks against known data structure or clinically meaningful relationships.

Question. Rapid Technical Questions to Rehearse

Difference between WHERE and HAVING?

WHERE filters rows before aggregation; HAVING filters groups after aggregation.

INNER JOIN vs LEFT JOIN?

INNER JOIN keeps matching records in both tables; LEFT JOIN retains all records from the left table and matched records from the right.

How do you detect duplicates?

Define the expected unit/key first, then count records by key and investigate keys with counts greater than one.

What is join cardinality?

The expected relationship between keys across tables - one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-one or many-to-many. Checking it prevents accidental row multiplication.

Missing data approach?

First determine amount, pattern, mechanism and importance; compare complete/incomplete records; then choose transparent handling such as complete-case, indicator/weighting or imputation where justified.

Statistical significance vs practical significance?

A small effect can be statistically significant in a large dataset but operationally unimportant. Interpretation should consider effect size, uncertainty, context and decision relevance.

When use qualitative data?

When understanding experience, implementation, mechanisms or barriers requires context that routine quantitative data cannot provide.

What is a logic model?

A representation of how program inputs and activities are expected to produce outputs and short-, intermediate- and long-term outcomes.

Process vs outcome evaluation?

Process evaluation asks whether/how implementation occurred; outcome evaluation asks whether intended changes occurred.

What is an interrupted time series?

A quasi-experimental approach that examines whether the level or trend of an outcome changes after an intervention while accounting for the pre-intervention trajectory.
