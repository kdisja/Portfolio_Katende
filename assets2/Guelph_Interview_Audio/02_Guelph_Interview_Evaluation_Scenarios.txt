Question. How would you evaluate a new Student Wellness program?

I would begin with the decision the evaluation needs to support rather than starting with the available data. First, I would work with the program team and relevant stakeholders to understand the intervention, its objectives, target population and theory of change. I would develop or review a logic model connecting inputs and activities to outputs and intended short- and longer-term outcomes.

Second, I would translate that into specific evaluation questions. For a mental-health initiative, for example, we might ask whether the intended students are being reached, whether access or wait times have improved, whether students report improved outcomes, and whether access and outcomes differ across student populations.

I would then identify indicators and data sources. Depending on the program, that could include administrative service data, program records, surveys and qualitative feedback from students or providers. I would assess data quality and establish an appropriate baseline or comparison. The design could range from descriptive monitoring to pre/post analysis, interrupted time series or another quasi-experimental approach, depending on implementation and data availability.

Equity would be built into the evaluation from the beginning. I would examine who is accessing the program relative to the population experiencing need, not simply compare raw counts. Finally, I would translate the findings into recommendations and an appropriate knowledge product - for example, an operational dashboard, evaluation report or concise leadership briefing.

My overall sequence is: understand the program, clarify the decision, define evaluation questions, establish indicators, assess the data, analyse rigorously, interpret with stakeholders, and translate the evidence into action.

Follow-up question. What if there is no baseline?

I would first ask what credible comparison can still be constructed. Options might include historical administrative data, a phased rollout, a comparable service or population, repeated post-implementation measures, or an interrupted time-series design if enough pre-intervention observations exist. If none is defensible, I would be explicit that the evaluation can assess implementation and outcomes but has limited causal attribution.

Follow-up question. What outcomes would you use?

I would derive them from the program's theory of change. For Student Wellness, I would separate process measures such as reach, wait time and completion from outcomes such as student-reported improvement, functioning, satisfaction or appropriate service-specific clinical outcomes. I would also examine equity in both access and outcomes.

Follow-up question. How would you incorporate qualitative data?

I would use qualitative data to answer questions the administrative data cannot: why students do or do not access a service, how they experience it, and what implementation barriers providers observe. I would integrate those findings with quantitative patterns so the evaluation explains not only what changed, but also plausible mechanisms and context.

Follow-up question. How would you assess causality?

I would match the strength of the causal claim to the design. Where randomization is not feasible, I would look for a credible counterfactual through approaches such as controlled before-and-after designs, interrupted time series or other quasi-experimental methods. I would examine alternative explanations, secular trends and confounding, and be explicit about residual limitations.

Question. Scenario: Counselling utilization increased by 30% this year. Leadership asks you to explain why. What would you do?

My first step would be not to assume that a 30% increase in utilization means underlying mental-health need increased by 30%. I would first validate the indicator and understand exactly what it represents: visits or unique students, all services or one service, and whether definitions, systems or reporting completeness changed.

Once I was confident the signal was real, I would decompose it. I would examine unique students versus repeat visits, service type, referral pathways, wait times, capacity, academic calendar and seasonality. I would also use an appropriate denominator because raw counts could be misleading if enrolment or the population eligible for the service changed.

Next, I would examine whether the increase is concentrated in particular services or student populations, while protecting confidentiality and avoiding unstable estimates from very small cells. I would then investigate context: new services, expanded hours, outreach campaigns, changes in referral practices or other operational changes that could increase access.

Only after those checks would I assess whether the pattern is consistent with increasing underlying need. I would present leadership with the most plausible explanations, evidence supporting each, key limitations, and any additional data needed to distinguish between competing explanations.

Follow-up question. What dashboard metrics would you show?

I would start with unique students served, visit volume, new versus returning users, wait time, service completion, referral source, capacity, no-shows or cancellations, selected outcomes, and equity indicators. I would show trends and denominators, not just current counts, and keep the top-level dashboard focused on metrics tied to decisions.

Follow-up question. How would you distinguish demand from access?

I would triangulate utilization with indicators of unmet need and service capacity. Increased visits could reflect increased need, but also expanded hours, better awareness or easier access. I would look at waitlists, wait times, referral volumes, appointment availability, survey measures of need, and the proportion of students seeking versus receiving care.

Follow-up question. What denominator would you use?

It depends on the question. For overall reach, the enrolled or otherwise eligible student population may be appropriate. For service uptake, I may need the population with an identified need or referral. I would define the denominator before interpreting the numerator and use subgroup-specific denominators for equity comparisons.

Question. Tell us about your experience with large institutional health datasets.

At CAREX Canada and BCCDC, I have worked with large linked Canadian population and health datasets, including Canadian Census Health and Environment Cohorts, cancer registry data, national surveys and administrative information.

The challenge with these data is not simply volume. Different sources have different structures, variable definitions, coding systems, time periods and completeness. I start by understanding the data-generating process and developing clear specifications for how variables and outcomes should be harmonized. I profile the data, assess keys and join cardinality, examine missingness and duplicates, standardize definitions and perform reconciliation checks after integration.

For cancer outcomes, I have worked with standardized ICD coding to ensure that outcomes are consistently defined before analysis. I have then used methods including survival and multivariable analysis in R and SAS.

Because these are sensitive linked data, the work also requires secure research environments, privacy controls and careful output review. This experience would transfer well to Student Wellness, where administrative, survey and program data may come from multiple systems and need to be integrated carefully before they can support reliable decisions.

Follow-up question. How do you validate a join?

Before joining, I establish the expected unit of observation and key cardinality in each table. I check uniqueness and duplicates, perform the join, then reconcile row counts, matched and unmatched records, and key variables before and after. I also spot-check records and investigate unexpected many-to-many relationships rather than assuming a technically successful join is correct.

Follow-up question. How do you deal with conflicting definitions?

I trace each definition back to its source, purpose and time period, then work with data owners to establish the operational definition needed for the analysis. I create a data dictionary or mapping, document any harmonization rules, and avoid silently treating similarly named variables as equivalent.

Follow-up question. What is your experience with administrative data?

My Canadian work has involved linked census, survey, cancer-registry and administrative health information in secure environments. That required understanding data provenance, harmonizing definitions and coding, validating linkages, handling missingness and using the data only within approved governance and disclosure rules.

Question. How do you ensure data quality and consistency across multiple systems?

I treat data quality as part of the entire analytical lifecycle rather than a one-time cleaning exercise. I begin by establishing expectations: record counts, unique identifiers, permissible values, date ranges, variable definitions and relationships between tables.

I then assess completeness, duplicates, missingness, outliers, inconsistent coding and logical inconsistencies. When combining systems, I explicitly check join cardinality and reconcile counts before and after joins because an incorrect many-to-many join can produce plausible-looking but incorrect results.

I also examine changes over time. A sudden increase in an indicator could represent a real change, but it could also reflect a new reporting definition, a system migration, expanded service capacity or improved data completeness. I document transformations and assumptions and, where possible, automate reproducible validation checks.

My guiding principle is that a sophisticated statistical model cannot compensate for poorly understood data. Before interpreting a result, I want to understand where the data came from, what they actually measure and what limitations they carry.

Follow-up question. Give us an example of a data-quality problem you found.

In linked Canadian health-data work, I encountered a recoding issue that produced results inconsistent with the underlying clinical and epidemiological context. I traced the variable back through the source documentation, corrected the transformation, reran the affected analysis and added explicit validation checks. The lesson was to validate meaning, not just data type and range.

Follow-up question. How do you document definitions?

I maintain a clear data dictionary and analysis specification showing source variable, operational definition, coding, derivation, permissible values and any transformation. For derived indicators, I document the numerator, denominator and exclusions. I also keep code versioned so the implemented definition is reproducible.

Follow-up question. What would you do if two systems disagree?

I would not arbitrarily choose one. I would determine whether the systems measure the same event, examine timing and update rules, identify the system of record where one exists, quantify the disagreement, and consult the data owners. The reconciliation rule and any unresolved discrepancy would be documented.

Question. How would you incorporate equity into Student Wellness evaluation?

I would treat equity as part of the evaluation design rather than an analysis added at the end. The first question is whether the program is reaching the populations it intends to serve relative to underlying need, not simply whether one group has more visits than another.

That means choosing appropriate denominators, examining representation and missingness, and where data permit, comparing access, wait times, service completion and outcomes across relevant student populations. I would be careful with small cells and unstable estimates, both for privacy and interpretation.

Quantitative differences also need context. Where appropriate, I would complement administrative or survey data with qualitative evidence and stakeholder engagement to understand barriers that may not be visible in routine data.

Finally, I would avoid framing group differences as deficits within students themselves. The purpose would be to identify potentially modifiable barriers in access, service design or institutional processes and translate those findings into practical improvements.

Follow-up question. What if demographic data are incomplete?

I would first quantify and characterize the missingness rather than treating 'unknown' as a neutral category. I would assess whether missingness differs by service, time or other observed characteristics, be cautious about subgroup comparisons, and clearly report the limitation. I would also work upstream on improving responsible demographic data collection if the information is important for equity monitoring.

Follow-up question. How would you report small groups?

I would prioritize both confidentiality and interpretability. Depending on policy and context, I might suppress counts, aggregate time periods or categories, report ranges or qualitative findings, and avoid cross-tabulations that enable re-identification. I would explain that absence from a table does not mean the group's experience is unimportant.

Follow-up question. What does an equitable service look like?

An equitable service is not necessarily one where every group uses it at the same rate. It is one where access, quality and outcomes are responsive to differing levels of need and where avoidable barriers are minimized. I would therefore look at need, reach, timeliness, experience and outcomes together.
