TOUCHSTONE INSTITUTE INTERVIEW PRACTICE
Manager, Data and Evaluation
Part 2 — Data Quality and Leadership

QUESTION 5: How do you ensure data quality and accuracy?

"I treat data quality as a governance problem, not a cleaning problem — most errors originate upstream, in definitions and collection, not in the analysis.

In the air pollution and cancer study I was linking national household surveys, cancer registries, and multiple Census cohorts in Statistics Canada's secure environment. The quality work was standardised ICD-coded definitions for eight cancer outcomes, linkage validation across datasets, and documented data dictionaries so the definitions were reproducible. I attached confidence ratings to each variable — validated, indicative, or imputed — so downstream analysts knew how much weight to place on it.

In the NTD program the challenge was different, because quality was distributed. With 2 coordinators and a statistician in each of 8 districts, the failure mode is that everyone is technically collecting 'coverage' but nobody means the same thing by it. So I standardised the definitions and the reporting template centrally, ran a regular cadence with the district statisticians, and reviewed outputs against the standard. Then I ran sensitivity checks on alternative spatial specifications to confirm the patterns weren't artefacts of boundary choices.

The principle I carry is make quality visible — a scorecard, a confidence rating, a sensitivity check. Anything that stops a rough estimate being mistaken for a validated one." ---

QUESTION 6: How do you present data to non-technical stakeholders?

"I start from the decision, not the data. Before I build anything I ask what choice the person needs to make and what's the smallest amount of information that supports it.

The NTD dashboard is the clearest example. The program team didn't need a statistical report on spatial autocorrelation — they needed to know which communities to prioritise for the next round of microplanning. So the dashboard led with a map of coverage gaps and a ranked list of priority communities, with the statistical detail available but not foregrounded. The analysis can be sophisticated; the interface has to be decision-ready.

The same applied internally. The district coordinators weren't analysts — they were the people closest to collection — so the reporting templates had to be usable without a statistics background. Getting that right is what made the central standardisation actually stick, rather than being quietly worked around.

In written work I use a one-page narrative: what we found, what it means, what we recommend. I test every visual on a non-technical colleague first. And I never strip the caveats to make it cleaner — I'll put them in a footnote or appendix, but I won't let a leader repeat a number I know is shakier than the slide implies." ---

QUESTION 7: Tell us about managing competing priorities.

"S: Running the data function across 8 districts, I was managing survey collection, the spatial analysis, and a funder reporting deadline at the same time — with a distributed team I couldn't see directly.

Task: Protect the critical path without dropping anything that mattered, and without losing quality in districts I wasn't physically in.

Action: Three moves. I mapped every deliverable to effort and to who was waiting on it, then had explicit conversations with stakeholders about what would move — I renegotiated one deadline rather than quietly missing three. I standardised the district reporting templates so coordinators weren't rebuilding formats each cycle. And I ran a weekly cadence with the district statisticians so I heard about problems early rather than at the deadline.

Result: The funder deliverable landed on time, the re-scoped work was agreed rather than imposed, and standardising the templates cut recurring effort substantially. The lesson: 'competing priorities' is usually a communication problem before it's a capacity problem — and in a distributed team it's also a visibility problem, which is why the cadence mattered more than the plan." ---

QUESTION 8: How do you lead a team, and how would you handle underperformance?

"I led the data function for the onchocerciasis program across 8 districts — 2 data coordinators and a statistician in each, roughly 24 people across a distributed team. I was accountable for the standards, the timelines, and the quality of everything coming out of those districts.

How I ran it: standardise, cadence, capability. Standardise — one data dictionary and one set of protocols, because inconsistency between sites is the thing that breaks first in a distributed team. Cadence — a regular working session with the district leads so problems surfaced early rather than at reporting time. Capability — I worked directly with the coordinators on data quality practice, because in a distributed function the person closest to collection sets the ceiling on quality. Several of them took on more analytical responsibility over the course of the program.

On underperformance, my approach is to name it early and specifically, and to work out whether it's a skill gap, a clarity gap, or a will gap — because the response differs. A skill gap gets training and scaffolding. A clarity gap is my failure, not theirs. A will gap gets a direct conversation with explicit expectations, a timeline, and documented follow-up. What I won't do is let it drift, because the kindest thing is to be clear while there's still time to fix it.

The one place I'd be precise: if accurate, I haven't owned the full formal HR cycle, like hiring and annual performance reviews, so I'd want to understand how much of that sits with this role versus your HR function." ---
